Physics Study Notes: Mechanics & Newton's Laws
1. Newton's First Law of Motion — Inertia
Definition: An object remains at rest or continues moving with constant velocity in a straight line unless acted upon by a nonzero net external force.
Key concepts
- Inertia is an object's resistance to changes in its state of motion.
- Mass measures an object's inertia.
- A larger mass requires a larger net force to produce the same acceleration.
- An object moving at constant velocity has zero net force acting on it.
Example
A 10 kg crate rests on a frictionless floor. A person pushes it with a force of 20 N.
If the crate is initially at rest, it begins accelerating because the net force is nonzero.
If the pushing force is removed and no other horizontal forces act, the crate continues moving at constant velocity.
Important distinction
Zero net force does not necessarily mean zero velocity. It means zero acceleration.
2. Newton's Second Law — Force and Acceleration
Definition: The net external force on an object equals its mass multiplied by its acceleration.
\[
F_{\text{net}} = ma
\]
Where:
- \(F_{\text{net}}\) = net force in newtons (N)
- \(m\) = mass in kilograms (kg)
- \(a\) = acceleration in metres per second squared (m/s²)
Example 1: Calculate acceleration
A 10 kg crate experiences a net force of 50 N.
\[
a = \frac{F_{\text{net}}}{m}
\]
\[
a = \frac{50}{10} = 5\text{ m/s}^2
\]
Answer: The crate accelerates at 5 m/s² in the direction of the net force.
Example 2: Compare two objects
Two crates experience the same net force of 40 N.
- Crate A has a mass of 5 kg.
- Crate B has a mass of 20 kg.
For crate A:
\[
a_A = \frac{40}{5} = 8\text{ m/s}^2
\]
For crate B:
\[
a_B = \frac{40}{20} = 2\text{ m/s}^2
\]
The lighter crate accelerates four times as much.
Rules
- Increasing net force increases acceleration when mass is constant.
- Increasing mass decreases acceleration when net force is constant.
- Acceleration points in the direction of the net force.
3. Newton's Third Law — Action and Reaction
Definition: When one object exerts a force on another object, the second object simultaneously exerts an equal-magnitude, opposite-direction force on the first.
Example
A person pushes a heavy crate with a force of 100 N.
The crate exerts a force of 100 N in the opposite direction on the person.
These forces act on different objects, so they do not cancel each other on a single object's free-body diagram.
Important concepts
- The forces are equal in magnitude.
- The forces point in opposite directions.
- They act on different objects.
- They occur simultaneously.
Common misconception
A heavy object does not exert a greater action force merely because it has greater mass. The interaction forces between two objects are equal in magnitude.
4. Friction
Definition: Friction is a force that opposes relative motion or the tendency for relative motion between contacting surfaces.
Types of friction
Static friction: Prevents relative sliding between surfaces.
Kinetic friction: Acts when surfaces slide relative to one another.
Kinetic friction formula
For an object sliding on a horizontal surface:
\[
f_k = \mu_k N
\]
Where:
- \(f_k\) = kinetic friction force
- \(\mu_k\) = coefficient of kinetic friction
- \(N\) = normal force
For a level surface with no other vertical forces:
\[
N = mg
\]
Therefore:
\[
f_k = \mu_k mg
\]
Example
A 10 kg crate slides across a horizontal floor. The coefficient of kinetic friction is 0.2. Use \(g = 10\text{ m/s}^2\).
Normal force:
\[
N = 10 \times 10 = 100\text{ N}
\]
Friction:
\[
f_k = 0.2 \times 100 = 20\text{ N}
\]
If the crate is pushed horizontally with a force of 50 N:
\[
F_{\text{net}} = 50 - 20 = 30\text{ N}
\]
Therefore:
\[
a = \frac{30}{10} = 3\text{ m/s}^2
\]
Answer: The crate accelerates at 3 m/s².
Important distinction
Static friction is not always equal to its maximum possible value. It adjusts as necessary to prevent sliding, up to its limit.
5. Weight and Normal Force
Weight
Weight is the gravitational force acting on an object.
\[
W = mg
\]
For an object with a mass of 20 kg, using \(g = 10\text{ m/s}^2\):
\[
W = 20 \times 10 = 200\text{ N}
\]
Normal force
The normal force is the contact force exerted perpendicular to a surface.
For an object resting on a horizontal surface, with no other vertical forces and no vertical acceleration:
\[
N = mg
\]
However, the normal force is not always equal to the object's weight. It can differ on inclined surfaces or when other forces or vertical accelerations are present.
6. Work and Energy
Mechanical work
For a constant force acting along the direction of displacement:
\[
W = Fs
\]
More generally:
\[
W = Fs\cos\theta
\]
Where:
- \(W\) = work in joules (J)
- \(F\) = force in newtons
- \(s\) = displacement in metres
- \(\theta\) = angle between the force and displacement
Example
A person pushes a crate with a constant horizontal force of 30 N over 4 m.
\[
W = 30 \times 4 = 120\text{ J}
\]
The person performs 120 J of work on the crate.
Kinetic energy
\[
KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2
\]
Gravitational potential energy
Near Earth's surface:
\[
PE = mgh
\]
Example
A 5 kg crate is lifted vertically by 2 m. Use \(g = 10\text{ m/s}^2\).
\[
\Delta PE = mg\Delta h
\]
\[
\Delta PE = 5 \times 10 \times 2 = 100\text{ J}
\]
The crate gains 100 J of gravitational potential energy.
7. Levers and Torque
This is particularly useful for testing your Impossible Machine game.
What is a lever?
A lever is a rigid structure that rotates around a pivot, also called a fulcrum.
A lever can help balance or move a load by applying forces at different distances from the pivot.
Torque
Torque describes the rotational effect of a force around a pivot.
For a perpendicular force:
\[
\tau = Fd
\]
Where:
- \(\tau\) = torque in newton-metres (N·m)
- \(F\) = applied force in newtons
- \(d\) = perpendicular distance from the pivot to the force's line of action
More generally:
\[
\tau = rF\sin\theta
\]
Rotational equilibrium
For a lever in static rotational equilibrium:
\[
\sum \tau = 0
\]
For two opposing perpendicular forces:
\[
F_1d_1 = F_2d_2
\]
If the forces are produced by stationary masses in a uniform gravitational field:
\[
m_1gd_1 = m_2gd_2
\]
Since \(g\) is the same on both sides:
\[
m_1d_1 = m_2d_2
\]
Example
A 20 kg load sits 1 m from a pivot.
A person applies a downward force on the opposite side, 2 m from the pivot.
Use \(g = 10\text{ m/s}^2\).
Load force:
\[
F_1 = 20 \times 10 = 200\text{ N}
\]
Load torque magnitude:
\[
\tau_1 = 200 \times 1 = 200\text{ N·m}
\]
For equilibrium:
\[
F_2 \times 2 = 200
\]
\[
F_2 = 100\text{ N}
\]
Answer: A downward force of 100 N at the opposite side balances the load, assuming the lever's own weight is negligible.
Important distinction
- The pivot is the point about which the lever rotates.
- The centre of mass is the weighted average position of an object's mass distribution.
- Torque measures the rotational effect of a force.
- These are three different concepts.
