# History Notes — The July Crisis and the Outbreak of the First World War

## 1. Long-term background

By 1914 Europe was divided into two alliance blocs. Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy formed the Triple Alliance. France and Russia were allied, and Britain had looser understandings with both (the Triple Entente). The alliances did not make war certain, but they meant that a local quarrel could draw in the great powers.

Nationalism in the Balkans was a constant source of tension. Austria-Hungary annexed Bosnia-Herzegovina in October 1908, which angered Serbia, because many Bosnians were Serbs. The Balkan Wars of 1912–1913 doubled Serbia's territory and made Austria-Hungary see Serbia as a direct threat to its empire.

Militarism shaped how leaders thought. Germany's war plan, the Schlieffen Plan, assumed that Germany would have to fight France and Russia at the same time. It planned to defeat France quickly by marching through neutral Belgium, before Russia's slow mobilisation was complete. This meant that for Germany, Russian mobilisation was effectively a reason to attack France at once.

## 2. The trigger

On 28 June 1914, Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne, was assassinated in Sarajevo by Gavrilo Princip, a Bosnian Serb linked to the Serbian nationalist group the Black Hand.

Common misconception: "The assassination caused the war on its own." The assassination was the trigger; it became a European war because of the decisions that followed it over the next five weeks.

## 3. The chain of decisions

- **5–6 July:** Germany gave Austria-Hungary the "blank cheque" — a promise of unconditional support for whatever action it took against Serbia. Without this promise Austria-Hungary was unlikely to risk war with Russia, Serbia's protector.
- **23 July:** Confident of German backing, Austria-Hungary sent Serbia an ultimatum with demands designed to be almost impossible to accept in full.
- **25 July:** Serbia accepted most of the demands but refused the one that would let Austro-Hungarian officials investigate inside Serbia.
- **28 July:** Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia.
- **30 July:** Russia, determined to protect Serbia, ordered general mobilisation.
- **1 August:** Germany treated Russian mobilisation as a threat and declared war on Russia.
- **3 August:** Following the Schlieffen Plan, Germany declared war on France.
- **4 August:** German troops invaded Belgium. Because Britain had guaranteed Belgian neutrality in the Treaty of London (1839), Britain declared war on Germany the same day.

Common misconception: "Britain went to war because of the alliance with France." Britain had no binding military alliance with France; the invasion of Belgium was the decisive reason given for Britain's entry.

## 4. How the causes connect

Historians separate long-term causes (alliances, militarism, imperial rivalry, Balkan nationalism) from the short-term trigger (the assassination) and the decisions of July 1914. Each decision in the July Crisis depended on the one before it: the blank cheque made the ultimatum possible, the rejected demand gave Austria-Hungary its reason for war, the declaration of war on Serbia led to Russian mobilisation, Russian mobilisation set the Schlieffen Plan in motion, and the Schlieffen Plan's route through Belgium brought Britain in.
